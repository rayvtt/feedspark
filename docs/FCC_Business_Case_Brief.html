01
The same task, keyed three times

Plan, brief, market status — one piece of work, three systems, by hand. Nothing looks wrong until one of the three is missed.

02
No single view of task volume

Emails, plans, scheduled work, calls and briefs — never summarised or categorised together. We cannot tell a client what we are carrying this month without rebuilding it.

03
Briefs not tracked end to end

Test and QA especially: what is running, what ASPL confirmed, what the numbers were. A result arrives in a reply and never reaches the plan.

04
Feed data not monitored

Custom labels and product types decide how PMAX splits the catalogue — spot-checked one feed at a time. A value empties; campaigns keep bidding into a structure that no longer matches.

05
No new-product tracking

Nothing said how many products arrived this month, or that a collection had landed unoptimised. New products sell on old titles until somebody notices.

And underneath all five, the same actions every week

Each is one action with different inputs — the definition of something a person should not be doing.

None of this is fixed by trying harder. Information workers who work like this have been measured for twenty years, and the findings do not move.

02 — What it costs

Five ways an unseen problem turns into money

Each of these happened on the book. None was anybody’s mistake — each was simply invisible until it was expensive.

01

A custom label empties on Monday

Revenue

Performance Max listing groups key on the exact values in the feed. One re-export with a label blank and every group built on it stops matching — the products still there, still approved, and quietly outside the structure that bids on them.

Without the FCCFound at the next spot-check, or when the client asks why that group’s spend fell away.
With itEvery feed read four times a day against a known-good baseline; the alert arrives with the client email already drafted.
02

The landing page changes price, the feed doesn’t

Revenue

A brand flips its default product option — subscription to single purchase — for Black Friday, and back four days later. A feed still carrying the old price is a price-mismatch disapproval, in the busiest fortnight of the year.

Without the FCCMerchant Center tells us, days later, by suspending the products.
With itBoth the flip and the revert sit on the keyword calendar as feed facts, with the revert named as the step most likely to be forgotten.
03

Three months of skipped optimisation, found at the review

Retention

The content team schedules keyword and title work weekly; the account manager answers go-ahead or skip. Every skip is reasonable in the week it is made, and nobody was counting the streak — 2,750 hours of scheduled work went unspent across the book.

Without the FCCThe account reads flat, and the first person to notice the pattern is the client.
With itSkip streaks per task and per brand, with the hours attached, on one screen — so it becomes a conversation, not a discovery.
04

378 hours delivered and never charged for

Revenue

Work carries on past the retainer because the relationship matters. Often the right call — but only when somebody makes it. Across the live book, 378 hours sat beyond the block, uncounted.

Without the FCCIt surfaces at renewal, as a cost overrun rather than as the case for a bigger retainer.
With itThe balance sits on every task, ticket and brand — with the team’s decision to keep going recorded beside it.
05

A brief that exists in one browser

Retention

A colleague raises two briefs, the emails go out, the delivery team picks them up — and the board still reads not briefed, because the save never reached the shared store.

Without the FCCThe work is invisible to everyone else. It gets chased, duplicated, or promised to the client a second time.
With itThe pipeline is the record: a failed save retries and says so on screen until it lands.

Three cost revenue, two cost trust, and none announces itself. That is the argument for a dashboard rather than more diligence — you cannot be more careful about something no screen is showing you.

05 — The team

Three mechanisms, not twenty modules

Each one takes a judgement out of the account manager's head — which is exactly what makes it an onboarding tool.

01

The bar is on the screen

What good looks like used to be learned from whoever had been here longest. 46 rules across the eight free-text fields, each quoting that attribute's own Google specification — so the same feed reads the same whoever opens it.

02

The next move is offered

Knowing what to do next used to be experience. Every brand ranks five next moves from nine deterministic rules, and each one opens its module already filtered to what it just counted.

03

The format is provided

A brief used to be as good as the person writing it. The composer scores six checks ASPL would otherwise come back and ask, and writes the tracking tokens itself.

A new account manager's first fortnight
1

Day one — read

One scoped invite. The dossier per brand: plan, feeds, hours, briefs. Feed questions answered in plain English.

2

Week one — act

First brief scored before it sends. A guard alert worked with the client email already drafted. Go or skip called with the balance in view.

3

Week two — own

Monday call prepared from Leadership. A scorecard exported to the client. A quote on the shared rate card.

And for whoever runs the team: coach from the same screen the AM sees, one standard applied evenly, and a live handover document per brand — so the floor rises while the ceiling is still the account manager.

Said plainly: this shortens the mechanical half of the apprenticeship — where the data lives, what good looks like, what a brief must contain. Not the relational half. Which is why every figure states its source and every alert opens to its evidence.
06 — Going forward

Ninety days to make it how the team works

Days 0–30 everyone in and a baseline diary measured · 30–60 every brief, quote and deck generated from it · 60–90 the diary re-run, the result reported, the next team chosen. Nothing in the first thirty days needs a developer.
