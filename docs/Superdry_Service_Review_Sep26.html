Seven markets carry a retainer. Twelve do not. Forty-two hours a month are contracted, all of it against the seven. The other twelve — both Belgian markets, both Canadian, both Swiss, Denmark, Spain, Finland, Italy, Norway and Sweden — are maintained, monitored and rule-managed at no charge, because a Superdry catalogue that works in one market and breaks in another is not a service anybody wants to sell.

One catalogue, nineteen localisations

Each market runs its own instance of the same ~58,800-product range with its own language, currency, shipping, category tree and label architecture. The UK alone carries 221 active rules; the estate carries 1,774, plus 675 held in reserve for seasonal work.

Every one of those rules runs on every import, every day, without anyone touching it. That is what a product arriving tomorrow inherits automatically.

What the rules actually do

They are not cosmetic. On the UK feed alone, rules build or rewrite the title, brand, category tree, colour, MPN, availability, shipping country, price and service, the sale and gender custom labels, range-completion stock logic, hero sizes, the Meta category, the Facebook and overlay image URLs, the Google product URL, UTM tagging, the display-ads fields, the Wunderkind title and class, and — since April — the two Local Inventory Ads pickup fields.

203 of the UK's 210 rules are impacting products today. The seven that are not are seasonal: Black Friday messaging and sale-phrase appends, dormant until November.

Revenue is never added across currencies here. Impressions and conversions are, because they are counts. Every market returned between 8× and 10× on ad spend over the year, and Germany — on an eight-hour retainer — returned the most.

Outerwear is the engine

In the UK over the last thirty days, Women's Jackets and Coats — 5,006 SKUs — returned £274,554 on £24,279 of spend. That is a ROAS of 1,130% from 8% of the catalogue.

Bomber jackets alone read 1,945%. This is where feed quality pays fastest, and it is where the keyword work has been aimed.

Womenswear outperforms

UK women's ROAS is 1,002% against men's 805%, on a similar SKU count — 28,896 against 26,204. Germany shows the same shape.

The gap is not budget. It is that the women's range carries deeper category detail and more of the keyword work has landed there.

And the half that earns nothing

Between 47% and 58% of each market's catalogue took under 100 impressions in thirty days — 47.1% UK, 51.2% Germany, 48.8% France, 57.6% Netherlands.

That is the single largest opportunity in this account, and the work to address it is already written and waiting — chapter 10.

September is the low month for an outerwear business. The last thirty days annualise to roughly 40% of the year's actual figures in BOTH spend and revenue, which is seasonality rather than decline — so the year is quoted throughout and the 30-day figures are only used where they are labelled as such. Never annualise the 30-day numbers in front of the client.

Chapter three

The transformation, measured

Superdry supplies a 29-character title. The feed ships an 83-character one. Both numbers are in the same file, on the same products, this morning.

Every feed carries the supplied title beside the optimised one.

That makes the before and the after directly comparable — same products, same file, same moment, no sampling and no estimate.

What Superdry supplies

A real UK product, read from today's feed

"Knitted Rugby Henley Jumper"

27 characters. No brand, no gender, no colour, no size, no fit. Across the UK catalogue the source title averages 29.1 characters and 54.3% of them are under thirty.

What FeedSpark ships

The same product, the same file, the same moment

"Superdry & Co Women's Knitted Rugby Henley Jumper Navy / Cable Navy - Size: 10"

77 characters. Brand, sub-brand, gender, product, colour, colourway and size. The UK average is 83.1 characters — +54 characters per product, on all 25,699 of them.

Not one title Superdry supplies, in any market, reaches the band. Nearly two thirds of the English-market titles do. That gap is the service, stated as plainly as it can be stated: 100% of titles are rewritten in all six markets, and every rewritten title is longer than the one it replaced.

And it is not only the title

On the same UK feed, read this morning: the category tree is mapped on 100% of products and runs four levels deep on 93.1% of them, against 305 distinct paths. Google's own category is on 100%. Every product carries exactly two images. Sale and full-price labelling is on 100%, gender labelling on 100%, category labelling on 99.8%.

Out-of-stock products never reach the feed at all — the availability rule removes them, which is why every live SKU reads in stock.

What the same read shows is still open

Product highlights reach 29.4% of UK products; 18,143 carry none. Keyword enrichment has reached 41.0%. Descriptions average 550 characters and have never been enriched — the flag reads D:N on every product in every market.

These are not oversights. They are the parts of the build that were scoped and never funded, and they are what chapter 10 asks for.

Germany, France and the Netherlands each get a rewritten title — 71.4, 68.4 and 51.4 characters against a supplied 35.8, 34.6 and 33.3 — but the enrichment that takes an English title from 29 to 83 characters has never been built in German, French or Dutch. The number to plan against

Nine of thirteen controlled tests won on impressions. The median is +9.42% impressions and +8.54% clicks — not the headline figures, the middle of the distribution.

That is the return on a keyword pass over 500 products, repeated monthly. It is modest, it is real, and it is the only number in this deck with a control group behind it.

At scale it is larger

The three biggest batches ever run — Black Friday I and II at 10,000 products each and the Summer/Seasonal pass at 9,653 — returned +48.5%, +39.4% and +55.3% impressions.

These are single-group reads with no control, so seasonality is inside them. But three batches of that size all moving the same way is not noise.

Brand in the title depends on the category

Run on the same day in January, the same test won on T-shirts (+10.2% impressions, +27.8% clicks) and lost on dresses (−7.5%, −8.0%).

So it is not a rule, it is a setting per category — which is exactly the kind of finding a testing programme exists to produce, and it cannot be bought any other way.

Eight of the forty tests returned nothing at all. Five title-optimisation tests and three image-tagging tests each closed with "no performance data detected". The title work this deck opens with — the change from 29 characters to 83 — has therefore never been measured against a control. On the evidence of chapter 03 it is the largest single change made to this feed, and it is the one we cannot yet put a number on.
Three single-group results are in the archive on batches of 7, 21 and 45 products, returning +14,047%, +165% and +1,566% impressions. They are arithmetically true and statistically meaningless; they are deliberately excluded from every figure in this chapter. If Superdry has seen them quoted before, that is worth getting ahead of.

Where the plan and the feed agree

The project plan records 169 of 260 initiatives closed. The lanes it shows as nearly finished are the ones the live feed confirms: custom labels 16 of 18, keywords 20 of 29, imagery 20 of 30, channels 18 of 26.

The lanes it shows as barely started are also confirmed: data fields 1 of 5, product type 1 of 3. Those two are the content layer, and they are where the score is lost.

What the audit adds that the plan cannot

FeedHero's own scan, refreshed yesterday, flags 19,791 UK products (34%) with three highlights or fewer, 21,647 (37%) with no pattern, and 12,007 (21%) whose size type is in the title but not in the size field.

It also flags that no custom alerts are configured on this account. Nineteen markets and no monitoring rules — that is a gap on our side, and it is first on the roadmap.

What the search-term data says is missing. Of the top thousand terms Superdry ranks against, 578 (58%) do not appear in any product title and 313 (31%) return no match anywhere in the feed. "Jackets for men" draws 14,800 searches a month and misses every title. "Jacket in yellow" draws 8,100 and matches nothing in the catalogue at all. This is the measurable size of the keyword and title gap, in Superdry's own demand.

The foundation is genuinely done

Tier 1 is not a claim, it is a measurement: 100% of titles rewritten, 100% category coverage at 3–4 levels, colour, size, gender, age group, GTIN, MPN and item group all complete, two images on 99.98% of products.

Local Inventory Ads fields are already in the UK feed — pickup method and next-day SLA went live in April. The plumbing for the next tier is in place.

And the ceiling is real

question_and_answer, document_link, related_product, item_group_title, variant_option and popularity_rank are the only fields in Google's spec whose stated purpose is AI comprehension. Superdry carries zero of six, confirmed from the schema of every market's feed.

Two of the six — item_group_title and variant_option — need no new content at all. They restructure the 5,725 variant families and the colour and size data already in the feed.

Why this matters for an outerwear retailer specifically. A shopper asking an assistant for "a warm waterproof parka under £150 that runs true to size" is asking about fill, rating, fit and cut. Superdry's descriptions contain those answers today — 550 characters of them per product. What is missing is the structure that lets an assistant quote them instead of guessing, and the 58% of demand terms that never reach a title.

The proportion, stated plainly

Over the last twelve months the UK feed served 591.6 million impressions and returned £16.18m on £1.68m of media. The UK retainer over the same period was 192 hours.

Across all four reporting markets: 920.8 million impressions, 10.9 million clicks and 334,866 conversions — from 42 contracted hours a month across nineteen markets.

Feed management is the least expensive line in a Shopping programme and the one every other line depends on. When a title is wrong, the bid does not matter.

Where the account actually sits

The UK carries a −97.75 hour balance: 45.25 hours used against a 16-hour block this cycle, on top of 68.5 carried forward. Sixteen of nineteen markets are in deficit.

That has been a deliberate choice, taken to keep nineteen markets whole through an SFCC migration, a platform move and four peaks. It is not sustainable as a permanent arrangement and this review is the moment to say so.

The honest position: the work delivered is roughly double the work contracted, and the parts that were never funded — descriptions, highlights outside English, the European keyword layer — are exactly the parts the next two years of search will reward.

What the unpaid markets have cost. Twelve markets with no retainer have taken 388 hours since 2022 — imports monitored, 1,000 rules maintained, disapprovals cleared, two migrations carried through. Whatever shape a renewal takes, those twelve either need an allowance or need to come off the service. Keeping them unfunded is the only part of this arrangement nobody has ever agreed to.
Ray's Vendor Review note frames Superdry's own position: "the marketing team is smaller, spend is also [reduced]". So this chapter must argue value-per-hour rather than ask for more hours outright. The strongest version of the argument is the ratio — 42 contracted hours a month against 920.8 million impressions — and the second strongest is that the 648 absorbed hours are the reason nineteen markets are still whole. Do not open with the deficit; open with the return.

Three things we need from Superdry

1. Sign-off on the exclusion thresholds. Range completion and minimum stock units. Both have been scoped since spring and both address the half of the catalogue earning nothing.
2. Margin and return-rate data, and Analytics access. Three asks that have sat open across several cycles. Each one turns a structural feed into a commercial one.
3. A decision on the twelve unfunded markets. Either an allowance or a reduced scope — not a continuation of the current arrangement.

Sequencing logic

W1 and W2 come first because neither is blocked on anybody, and both protect Peak rather than change it. W4 needs one conversation. Everything structural lands before November.

W3, W5 and W6 sit in January deliberately. The German and French keyword layer is the largest unexploited return on this account, and it should be built and measured in a quiet quarter, not in Peak.

Chapter eleven

Ways of working

Forty-six people have worked on this account. Four carry it day to day.

01

Brief

Work is raised against a market and a category, with a scope, a due date and a definition of done.

02

Build

The optimisation team applies it as a rule, not an edit — so it keeps applying to every product that arrives after it.

03

Measure

Batches go live with a read-out date. Where a control group is possible, one is used.

04

Report

Results return to the archive this deck is built from — forty tests and counting.

The work that never appears as a win

A large share of any feed retainer goes on keeping things from breaking, and none of it produces an uplift number:

53 feeds kept in sync
1,774 rules maintained
19 markets of disapprovals
Two migrations carried through

443 hours of account management and 75 hours of disapproval clearance since 2022 — the cost of nineteen markets not going down.

Who carries it

Ray leads the account and has personally logged 469.5 hours on it. Steven Opuni is the executive AM. Day to day the optimisation work runs through a named team — Vitus, Gary, Lara, Febin, Matt and Murugan account for another 1,627 hours between them.

Forty-six people have touched this account across four years. That continuity is why a rule written in June 2022 still runs correctly today.

The through-line

Everything in this deck is one pattern: a 29-character title became an 83-character one, and it stayed that way without anybody touching it again. Nineteen markets, 1,774 rules, 920.8 million impressions. The parts that are not finished are not failures of delivery — they are the parts that were never funded, and they are the ones the next two years of search will reward most.
